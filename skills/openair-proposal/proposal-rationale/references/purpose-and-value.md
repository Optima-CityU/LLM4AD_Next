# Research Purpose, Significance, and Value Method

This stage answers two connected questions: why the research must be undertaken, and what credible value follows if it succeeds.

## 1. Construct the necessity argument

Build a narrowing chain:

1. **Context:** establish the relevant scientific or technical development using verified evidence.
2. **Specificity:** explain why the target problem differs from adjacent, already-studied problems.
3. **Limitation:** identify what current approaches cannot reliably explain or achieve under the proposal's conditions.
4. **Consequence:** show why that limitation matters scientifically or practically.
5. **Purpose:** state the bounded research purpose that responds to the limitation.

Avoid a generic background section followed by an unrelated project announcement. Each paragraph must advance the same argument.

Treat the relationship with the literature stage as iterative. If the intended
purpose depends on an unsupported comparison or limitation, report the exact
evidence gap and return to the literature stage rather than converting the
assumption into a factual claim.

## 2. Separate purpose from significance

The purpose states what understanding, method, or capability the project seeks to establish. Significance explains the consequences of that outcome:

- **theoretical significance:** what mechanism, relationship, or conceptual understanding becomes clearer;
- **methodological significance:** what research or technical approach becomes possible or more systematic;
- **practical significance:** what realistic decision, workflow, or deployment problem could be better addressed.

Use restrained language such as "may support", "can help", or "is expected to" when evidence does not justify certainty. Do not claim that the project has already achieved its intended result.

## 3. Develop academic and application value

For each value claim, use `problem -> project capability -> beneficiary or research consequence`:

1. name a concrete unresolved issue or operational constraint;
2. link it to a specific capability that the proposed research is designed to produce;
3. identify who or what benefits and under which conditions;
4. state limitations or dependencies that prevent overclaiming.

Academic value should follow from the literature gap. Application value should follow from plausible project outputs and real scenarios, not from broad statements about market importance.

## 4. Frame practical problems from the user's perspective

When the proposal has an applied component, describe a small set of distinct practical problems. For each:

- current setting and constraint;
- failure, cost, or risk created by the constraint;
- why the existing practice is insufficient;
- which proposed capability addresses it;
- what observable improvement would matter.

Cover distinct lifecycle stages or problem types only when the project actually addresses them.

## 5. Maintain section boundaries

- `01-rationale.typ` contains the research purpose, necessity, and significance argument.
- `03-value.typ` contains academic value, credible application prospects, and practical problems.
- Detailed literature synthesis remains in the literature section.
- Objective enumeration and technical procedures remain in their owning later stages.

## 6. Quality gate

Check that:

- every factual trend or prior-work claim uses an existing bibliography key;
- the stated gap matches the literature section rather than exaggerating it;
- the purpose preserves the foundation's scope and exclusions;
- each value claim maps to a planned output or capability;
- theory, method, and practice are distinct but mutually consistent;
- no institution, result, user need, or industry fact has been invented.
